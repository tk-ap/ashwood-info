Just because you can build it, should you?

    02WHY THIS MATTERS

    Building is abundant. Judgment about what deserves to exist is scarce.

    When software took months and cost a fortune, the decision to build carried its own discipline. That discipline is gone. What remains is a harder question, asked earlier.

    Enthusiasm is not evidence. It is easy to mistake your own excitement — or a few polite nods — for proof that a problem exists. Evidence is what people already do: what they pay for, work around, complain about, or ask for without being prompted. Until behavior shows up, you have a hypothesis, not a foundation.

    Software is often the most expensive answer. Many problems dissolve into a conversation, a document, a checklist, a service performed by hand, or a feature inside something people already use. Code adds maintenance, expectations, and trust obligations that persist long after the novelty fades. The cheapest version that proves value usually is not software at all.

    Distribution decides more than quality. A good tool nobody finds is indistinguishable from a tool that was never built. If you cannot name the first ten users and reach them directly, you do not have a software problem — you have a distribution problem, and building first only postpones facing it.

    Not building can be the strongest accomplishment. Every DON’T BUILD verdict protects weeks of effort, preserves trust with the people you would have served poorly, and keeps your attention available for the thing that actually deserves to exist. Deciding against software, with reasons you can state plainly, is building judgment — the one asset AI cannot produce for you.
