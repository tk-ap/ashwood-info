THE MIND IS THE MOAT

On AI, anti-intellectualism, human context, and why knowledge should create more participants—not more dependents.

September 2, 2026TK Ashwood

I’m building with AI at almost the exact moment I’m becoming increasingly uncomfortable with what AI is being used to make.

That probably sounds contradictory. It isn’t.

I think artificial intelligence is one of the most powerful creative and intellectual tools we have ever been given access to. I also think we are in danger of using it in a way that rewards the exact opposite of intelligence: more output with less thought, more content with less curiosity, more answers with fewer questions.

The conversation around “AI slop” usually focuses on aesthetics—ugly images, generic articles, lifeless marketing copy, thousands of nearly identical videos filling a feed. I don’t think slop is the real problem. Slop is evidence of something deeper.

What worries me is anti-intellectualism becoming automated.

There has always been pressure to avoid complexity—to prefer certainty over investigation, speed over rigor, familiarity over nuance. AI did not create that tendency. It just made it extraordinarily easy to scale.

A person can now produce an essay without really wrestling with the argument. Launch a website without understanding what it is supposed to communicate. Summarize a book they never read. Generate a brand without developing taste. Publish ten opinions before taking enough time to form one.

The technology is not demanding that we do this. The incentive structure is.

We live inside an economy that rewards production, distribution, attention, and growth: more things, delivered to more people, more frequently. Generative AI dramatically reduces the cost of all of them.

generate → distribute → measure → optimize → generate again.

Eventually machines are producing material designed to satisfy systems measuring reactions to material produced by other machines.

That is not intelligence. It is autonomous enshittification.

And yet I use AI every day.

That is the contradiction I’m actually interested in.

I am critical of capitalism while building companies inside of it. I dislike the instinct to turn every useful idea into an asset, every audience into a funnel, every improvement in efficiency into an excuse to produce more, and increasingly, every piece of knowledge into something that can be packaged, gated, monetized, or made artificially scarce.

I also have bills. Infrastructure costs money. Creative work costs money. Independence requires resources. Ownership matters. I do not know how to step outside of that system, and I’m not interested in pretending otherwise.

How do you participate in a system without allowing its incentives to determine the values of your work?

I think the same question applies to AI.

The interesting choice is not between using AI and rejecting it. It is between deciding what role the technology should play in the intellectual process and allowing convenience to decide for us.

There is a version of the future where AI progressively removes the human from the loop. I’m interested in almost the opposite—not because humans should manually perform every task forever, but because the human being possesses something extraordinarily difficult to manufacture:

context.

Not just information. The memory of why something matters. The experience that makes one detail important and another irrelevant. The contradictions in your own beliefs. The thing somebody said to you ten years ago that changes how you understand a problem today. Taste built from thousands of exposures you cannot individually name. Cultural knowledge. Family history. Regret. Ambition. Humor. Fear. Things you have not figured out how to articulate yet.

You can give a model an enormous context window. That does not mean you have given it your context.

This is where I think some of the popular framing around AI gets backward. Humans are often treated as the bottleneck—the slow component standing between a sufficiently capable machine and perfect execution.

I increasingly think the human is the scarce input.

AI can process an astonishing amount of information, but someone still has to determine what information matters. Someone has to notice what is missing. Someone has to recognize when an answer is technically competent but intellectually hollow. Someone has to decide whether the thing deserves to exist at all.

That does not make the machine less powerful. It makes context more valuable.

The loop I’m interested in looks less like:

prompt → generation → publish → repeat

and more like:

experience → context → judgment → AI amplification → evaluation → deeper context.

The machine participates. It can research, challenge, organize, simulate, write, code, execute, critique, and surface connections I might have missed. But the purpose is not to escape thinking.

The purpose is to extend how far thinking can go.

That distinction has started showing up in the things I’m building. I’m interested in AI that understands people more deeply rather than simply answering prompts more quickly. Systems where agents can act but authorization, evidence, and boundaries remain explicit. Tools that help one person accomplish more without pretending that person has become irrelevant. Technology that treats context as infrastructure rather than residue.

And that has brought me back to education.

My grandmother, Myrtle Alvira Ashwood, devoted her life to early childhood education. Her work concerned creativity and the environments in which learning happens. I'm returning to that now as we find ourselves in the midst of the most profound change in the learning environment of the 21st century—one where almost anyone may have access to a machine capable of explaining, researching, translating, generating, and helping them make things.

The question is not simply what these machines can teach us. It is what kind of learners we become beside them.

More curious, or more passive? More capable of asking better questions, or merely more accustomed to receiving faster answers? More empowered to participate, or increasingly dependent on systems we never bothered to understand?

I do not think the answer is to stand at a safe distance and complain about what other people are making with AI. I also do not think it is responsible to tell people not to worry about understanding any of it because the machine will eventually handle everything for them.

There is another option:

learn the medium.

You do not have to become a machine-learning engineer. You do not have to start an AI company. You do not have to discard the expertise you spent years earning.

Learn enough to understand what has changed. Learn enough to recognize what the technology does well and where it fails. Learn enough to discover what becomes possible when the new medium meets the context you already carry.

Then make something.

A teacher brings knowledge of how people actually learn. A nurse understands realities of care invisible to a software team. An artist brings taste shaped by the realities of their life. A parent brings acute observation and a deep connection to intuition. A researcher brings rigor, curiosity, and a willingness to challenge the status quo. A tradesperson brings embodied knowledge earned through untold hours of apprenticeship. A small-business owner understands workflows no benchmark has ever measured.

The tool may be new. Your knowledge is not obsolete.

Bring it with you.

And then teach somebody else.

There is an old literacy principle I keep returning to: each one, teach one. I like it because it refuses the idea that knowledge should terminate with the person fortunate enough to gain access to it.

Knowledge can support livelihoods. Teachers should be paid. Researchers should be funded. Writers should be compensated. Expertise has value, and the labor required to develop, test, translate, and teach that expertise has value too.

But knowledge itself cannot become valuable simply because other people are prevented from having it.

That distinction matters to me.

We already know what happens when access to education, information, technology, and institutional language becomes a mechanism of power. Entire systems have been built around deciding who gets to understand, who gets to participate, and who must remain dependent on someone else’s access.

AI gives us the opportunity to reproduce that pattern at extraordinary speed.

A small class of people could learn how to work effectively with increasingly capable machines, wrap that understanding in proprietary language, and sell everyone else permanent access to the gate.

Or we can do something else.

If a new capability becomes understandable to you, widen the circle. Explain the concept. Share the method when you can. Document the failure. Teach the person who assumes this world has nothing to do with them where their existing knowledge becomes useful.

The goal should not be to eliminate expertise. It should be to create more of it: more capable people, more independent thinkers, more makers who understand the tools they are using, more people able to question the systems being built around them, and more teachers who can help someone else cross the threshold.

One person gaining capability should create another participant, not another dependent.

Technological change is too often narrated as something that happens to us. A small group invents the future while everybody else waits to discover what role they have been assigned inside it.

I don’t believe we have to accept that relationship.

You can learn. You can question. You can experiment. You can contribute from the field you already know. You can help somebody else understand enough to begin.

You do not have to sit on the sidelines and watch the world change.

Some of what I build will become products. Some of it will become photographs, music, writing, research, lessons, or things I do not have names for yet. Some ideas will fail. Some will contradict things I believe today.

That is probably the point.

I do not want artificial intelligence to protect me from changing my mind. I want it to help me earn better reasons to change it.

Execution is getting cheaper. Information is abundant. Generation is becoming trivial.

That makes the things we were tempted to outsource first more important, not less: the question, the judgment, the context, the curiosity to learn, the responsibility to share.

The mind.

Each one, teach one.

THE MIND IS THE MOAT.
